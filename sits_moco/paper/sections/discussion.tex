\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

We discuss how MSTNet relates to~\citet{song2022soy}, how mean-then-encode training relates to encode-then-mean pixel evaluation, what the 2022 drought fold implies for operations, and where the present features, architecture, and losses may still fall short, with a few ideas for future work.

\subsection{Temporal versus spatial holdout}
\label{sec:discussion-yoo-vs-muni}

To the best of our knowledge, the closest counterpart in aim is~\citet{song2022soy}: they use Brazilian soybean yield at municipal scale from multi-year satellite, climate, and soil inputs under IBGE PAM labels, while keeping the model capable of predicting at the pixel level.
In the main results we focused on the temporal holdout.
As~\citet{song2022soy} also did, however, a spatial holdout is another valid way to measure what the model can do.
The two ask different questions.
A spatial split that holds out part of the (municipality, year) pairs tests whether the model can interpolate to unseen places under seasons already observed in training, which is useful for downscaling and gap-filling within known years.
Leave-one-year-out keeps the places and drops a calendar year, testing whether the model can generalize to an unseen season, closer to forecasting the current year.
Temporal holdout is typically harder because the model cannot rely on having already seen that year's climate regime, phenology, or yield level among the training municipality--years; spatial holdout can still borrow seasonal structure shared across the panel.

Very briefly on the numbers:~\citet{song2022soy} report spatial-holdout $R^2=0.69$ (RMSE $344$\,\si{kg/ha}) and leave-one-year-out RMSE from $259$ to $816$\,\si{kg/ha}, with a mean per-year leave-one-year-out $R^2$ of about $0.42$ across 2001--2019.
Under the same spatial split on Paran\'{a}, MSTNet reaches pooled $R^2=0.736$ (RMSE $0.448$\,\si{t/ha}; Table~\ref{tab:muni80-20}), with yearly holdout $R^2$ from $0.283$ to $0.636$ (mean $0.467$).
That puts our spatial performance roughly in line with theirs, at least on the spatial split.
The pooled score is also higher than the best single-year $R^2$ ($0.636$), which is the signature of strong between-year separation: when all harvest years sit in one pool, getting the seasonal level right across wet and dry years inflates $R^2$, whereas within a fixed year the model has less room to separate municipalities.
A caveat is the panel: we have only five harvest years in Paran\'{a}, whereas they observe nineteen years across many Brazilian states.
More years are clearly helpful for temporal coverage.
More states can help if neighbouring regions share yield-relevant structure, but can also add geographic and management complexity that a single model must absorb.

\subsection{Mean-then-encode versus encode-then-mean}
\label{sec:discussion-mean-vs-pixel}

Municipal-mean evaluation and the post-training pixel path are not the same estimator.
The former applies $f_\theta$ to soy-masked averages of the inputs (mean-then-encode), whereas the latter scores each soy pixel and averages the outputs (encode-then-mean).
Since $f_\theta$ is nonlinear, predicting from municipal means need not match the mean of pixel-wise predictions.
The slightly higher pooled $R^2$ on the pixel path suggests that post-training downscaling can recover municipal signal that is smoothed away when covariates are pre-averaged.

Training on the encode-then-mean path would align checkpoint selection with pixel inference and sub-municipal maps, but we kept municipal-mean training for practical reasons.
Each municipality--year is one training example (municipalities times training years per LOO fold), and the full fold fits in memory.
Pixel-wise training would run a forward and backward pass on every soy pixel in those municipality--years before municipal averaging, which amounts to hundreds of millions of pixel updates per epoch, rather than the tens of thousands of municipal updates per epoch.
Besides the extra wall-clock time of millions of additional forward and backward passes per epoch, the data themselves become an I/O bottleneck: the network weights fit comfortably in GPU memory, as well as the tens of thousands of the mean-pixel series, but at hundreds of millions of soy pixels the time series no longer fit and must be streamed from disk in batches.
Those costs made pixel-wise training too expensive relative to municipal-mean fitting, so we train mean-then-encode and report pixel-path LOO on the same checkpoints.

\subsection{The 2022 drought year}
\label{sec:discussion-drought-2022}

The 2022 fold is the exception that matters for generalizability evaluation.
It is the only drought year in the Paran\'{a} sample, so leaving it out removes every training example from that regime.
The resulting negative $R^2$ under both evaluation paths shows that the present covariates are not enough to learn drought behaviour from wetter years alone.
When 2022 remains in training and another year is held out, LOO $R^2$ is much more stable.
This gap is mainly a data problem.
If the training set includes municipalities that suffered poor yields in other years and regions, drought-like response patterns become observable rather than unique to one left-out season.\footnote{We have already begun that expansion by adding Rio Grande do Sul, S\~{a}o Paulo, and Goi\'{a}s to the municipal-mean pipeline. On that multi-state year LOO setup we obtain mean $R^2$ about $0.46$, with fold scores ranging from about $0.2$ to $0.7$. We do not report those numbers as main results here because the pixel-by-pixel pipeline has not yet been run at that scale, and municipal-mean scores alone would miss the downscaling objective that motivates MSTNet.}

Modality ablations on the same year-LOO folds show that climate carries most of the non-drought signal, Sentinel-2 less, and soil least.
Omitting climate costs about $0.32$ in mean pixel-path $R^2$ when 2022 is excluded, versus about $0.26$ without Sentinel-2 and about $0.07$ without soil.
Soil still matters for interpretation: texture and SOC are nearly static across years, so they act as a spatial productivity prior that helps typical seasons but can worsen the drought fold.
Without soil, the 2022 LOO score rises from $-0.422$ to $-0.203$, while ordinary years still favor the full three-stream model.
Ranking modalities by the mean that includes 2022 can therefore look misleading; the fair comparison for typical seasons is the mean that excludes that fold.

That same distinctiveness has the opposite effect under the spatial holdout: among the five seasons, 2022 has the highest per-year $R^2$ (Table~\ref{tab:muni80-20}).
Since drought-year municipality--years appear on both sides of the split, the model can learn the 2022 response from the training municipalities and use it to rank held-out places within that same season. The year is then no longer an unseen regime, only unseen municipalities inside a season already represented in training.

Paran\'{a} year-LOO makes 2022 look uniquely hard only because that drought is absent from training.
Once drought seasons are represented among the training years, we expect the opposite: drought years should be the easier folds, because climate and spectral inputs leave a strong imprint of stress that the model can map to yield collapse.
Favourable seasons are the harder case for within-year ranking: after the seasonal level is set, the residual gap between good and poor municipalities will have more to do with management, cultivar, and planting decisions that our covariates do not observe.

\subsection{Model limits}
\label{sec:discussion-encoder-decoder}
Two failure modes matter beyond the headline LOO scores.
First, the model can look competent by forecasting the year well while ranking municipalities badly inside that year.
Climate already explains most of the year-to-year swing on non-drought folds, so MSE rewards getting the seasonal level right; the smaller within-year gaps in PAM are easier to ignore.
Second, mid-range municipalities can collapse toward a common predicted value: under plain MSE alone there is little reason to spread predictions when within-year residuals are noisy, yet absolute level must still be calibrated across years.
Drought LOO stresses both failure modes at once, because the model must predict an unseen seasonal level and still order municipalities without any drought example in training.
A further ceiling is the covariates themselves: management, cultivar, planting cycle, and field history are missing, and soy-masked municipal means erase within-municipality contrast that might separate productive from marginal soy inside the same PAM unit.

\subsubsection{Architecture and losses}
\label{sec:discussion-architecture-losses}

As stated above, the model might be good at forecasting the year well while ranking municipalities badly inside that year.
We have a couple of ideas on how to update the architecture and losses to reward within-year ranking.

The simplest ``brute-force'' fix is to put the missing objective in the loss: within-year ranking or correlation terms, and a penalty when predictions inside a year are unrealistically tight.
Those terms ask who is above or below that year's mean and back-propagate through both the decoder and the embedding, so the same failure mode can be fixed in either place.
A year-level baseline plus a municipal deviation would separate seasonal calibration from within-year ranking more explicitly, and would make under-dispersion a measurable failure.

A generative auxiliary task might also enrich the embedding: it pressures input fidelity so trunks do not discard spectral or climate detail that a deep yield head does not need for a coarse seasonal fit. That keeps more of the input structure available for ranking, without itself teaching within-year order.

On the climate trunk encoder specifically, Seq2Vec today is a uniform mean over valid days, unlike the NDVI-weighted pool on the spectral side; a learned or phenology-aware climate Seq2Vec that up-weights dry or hot windows could make the climate embedding more sensitive to stress without changing daily inputs or the pixel-path layout.
Another way to make the embedding space richer is self-supervised pre-training of the trunks, as~\citet{xu2024sitsmoco} do for STNet on large Sentinel-2 archives before downstream fine-tuning. We train MSTNet from scratch on municipal means, so having the pre-training might be helpful.

\subsubsection{Features}
\label{sec:discussion-features}
A keen reader may notice that we did not engineer yield covariates from the streams. The spectral trunk receives Sentinel-2 band time series, the climate trunk receives daily Xavier variables, and soil enters as MapBiomas Solo attributes, without the vegetation indices, phenology summaries, or seasonal aggregates that are often used in the literature.
NDVI appears only as Seq2Vec pooling weights inside the spectral encoder, not as an input feature alongside the bands.
This choice was conscientiously made and the working assumption is that a multi-frequency Transformer can recover the nonlinear relationships that hand-crafted indices encode.
Although our expectation is that the MLP and the Transformer will recover all relevant information for the yield prediction task, in practice that recovery is not guaranteed. With only municipal PAM labels and an MSE that mostly rewards the seasonal level, the network may never be forced to form the same cues that hand-crafted features make explicit.
Passing standard indices and climate stress summaries as additional channels would test whether explicit phenology and stress cues still help when the raw series are already in the model.
